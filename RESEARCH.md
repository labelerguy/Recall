# Why Recall works the way it does

Every study rule in Recall comes from a peer-reviewed primary study: a real experiment, published in a journal, with the original paper or its official abstract checked. No blog posts, Wikipedia articles, or social media summaries. If a rule had no primary source behind it, it was taken out.

Each entry is marked with how it was checked:
- **Verified (full text)**: the original paper was read.
- **Verified (abstract)**: the journal or PubMed abstract was read.
- **Citation verified, finding not yet**: the paper exists, but the full text couldn't be opened in this pass. The finding is stated from the published work and should be checked against the paper before citing it anywhere.

Wikipedia is used in one place only: as a host for artwork photos on image cards. It isn't a source for any study rule.

## 1. Keep recalling a card after you get it right once
**What Recall does:** a card isn't finished the first time you get it right. Cards keep coming back until they reach their criterion (see #2).

**Study:** Karpicke, J. D., & Roediger, H. L. (2008). The critical importance of retrieval for learning. *Science, 319*, 966–968. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1152408. **Verified (full text).**

- **Design:** students learned 40 Swahili–English word pairs. In some conditions, a pair was dropped from further testing once it was recalled. In others, it stayed in.
- **Result:** during the session, learning looked identical in every condition. One week later, students recalled about **80%** when pairs kept being tested, versus **33–36%** when pairs were dropped from testing. Extra studying of already-recalled pairs added nothing; repeated retrieval made the difference.
- **Also:** students predicted about 50% recall in every condition, so they couldn't tell which method worked.

## 2. Recall to criterion, then relearn in later sessions
**What Recall does:** the first time you learn a card, it needs **3 correct recalls** in that session. In each later session, it needs **1**. A missed card returns after about 3 other cards. A correct-but-not-finished card returns after about 5.

**Study:** Rawson, K. A., & Dunlosky, J. (2011). Optimizing schedules of retrieval practice for durable and efficient learning: How much is enough? *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 140*, 283–302. **Citation verified, finding not yet** (from the authors' lab publication list).

- **Finding as summarized:** across three experiments (533 students), a higher initial criterion helped at first, but that advantage shrank as relearning sessions were added. Relearning sessions had large effects on retention 1 to 4 months later for little extra time.
- **Their recommendation:** 3 correct recalls initially, then relearning sessions spaced widely apart.

**Related applied study:** Rawson, K. A., Dunlosky, J., & Sciartelli, S. M. (2013). The power of successive relearning: Improving performance on course exams and long-term retention. *Educational Psychology Review, 25*, 523–548. **Citation verified, finding not yet.**

## 3. Space sessions according to how far away the exam is
**What Recall does:** set an exam date on a deck. After a card reaches criterion, its next session is scheduled at a fraction of the time left before the exam: about **30%** of the time left when the exam is a week out, shrinking to about **7.5%** when it's a year out. The last session always lands the day before the exam. With no exam date set, Recall plans for a test one month out.

**Study:** Cepeda, N. J., Vul, E., Rohrer, D., Wixted, J. T., & Pashler, H. (2008). Spacing effects in learning: A temporal ridgeline of optimal retention. *Psychological Science, 19*(11), 1095–1102. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x. **Verified (abstract).**

- **Result:** the best gap between study sessions grows with the time until the test. As a share of that time, it shrinks: roughly **20–40%** of a 1-week delay, down to **5–10%** of a 1-year delay.
- **How Recall uses it:** 30% and 7.5% are the midpoints of those two reported ranges. Values in between are a straight-line interpolation on a log scale. The interpolation is Recall's own assumption; the paper reports the endpoints, not a formula.

## 4. Mix cards instead of grouping them
**What Recall does:** every session shuffles the deck, so works from different periods and artists are mixed together instead of studied in blocks.

**Study:** Kornell, N., & Bjork, R. A. (2008). Learning concepts and categories: Is spacing the "enemy of induction"? *Psychological Science, 19*(6), 585–592. **Verified (full text).**

- **Design:** participants studied paintings by 12 artists, either grouped by artist or mixed with other artists. They were then tested on **new** paintings by the same artists.
- **Result:** accuracy was **.61 mixed vs .35 grouped** (Experiment 1a) and **.59 vs .36** (Experiment 1b).
- **Also:** 78% of participants did better with mixing, but 78% rated grouping as equally or more effective. That's directly relevant to identifying artworks on an exam.

## 5. Test yourself instead of rereading
**What Recall does:** Learn and Test both make you produce the answer before you see it. Flip cards stay available but are labeled as the weakest mode.

**Study:** Roediger, H. L., & Karpicke, J. D. (2006). Test-enhanced learning: Taking memory tests improves long-term retention. *Psychological Science, 17*(3), 249–255. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x. **Verified (abstract).**

- **Result:** restudying beat testing on a test 5 minutes later. Testing beat restudying substantially at **2 days** and **1 week**. Restudying also raised students' confidence without improving delayed retention.
- **Why Cram exists anyway:** it's for the last day only, where the short-term result applies. It doesn't change your schedule.

## 6. Predict your score before a test
**What Recall does:** the blank-page test asks for your predicted score first, then shows it next to your real score and keeps the history.

**Studies:**
- Karpicke & Roediger (2008), above: predictions didn't track actual recall.
- Kornell & Bjork (2008), above: people judged the worse method as better.

Both are verified. Showing the gap is Recall's design choice based on those findings. These studies didn't test the prediction feature itself.

## 7. Score every part of a multi-part answer against the real answer
**What Recall does:** for cards with several parts (name, date, culture, location, medium, fact), you check off each part you had. "Got it all" is locked until every part is checked. Otherwise the card counts as missed.

**Study:** Dunlosky, J., & Rawson, K. A. (2012). Overconfidence produces underachievement: Inaccurate self evaluations undermine students' learning and retention. *Learning and Instruction, 22*, 271–280. **Citation verified, finding not yet**: the abstract page blocked automated access.

- **Finding as published, to check against the paper:** students who judged partly-correct answers as correct stopped practicing those items too early and remembered less later. Scoring against the full correct answer made self-judgments more accurate.

## What was removed for lacking a primary source
- **The "Easy" button and fixed review intervals (1, 3, 7, 14, 30, 60 days).** Those are common in flashcard apps, but the specific numbers had no study behind them. They were replaced by #2 and #3.
- **Any "learning style" setting.** Matching material to a preferred style (visual, auditory) has not been supported in controlled tests. Recall has no such setting.

## Adding a new feature
A new study rule needs one peer-reviewed experiment with a comparison group, read in full text or official abstract, before it ships. Add it here with its citation, design, result, and verification status.
